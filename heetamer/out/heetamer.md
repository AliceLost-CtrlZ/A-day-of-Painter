# Heetamer

*people-land* — a survey of the country, its waters, and the names its three tongues have given them.

---

## The country

Heetamer occupies 689,955 square kilometres between 55° and 38° north. A single range runs the length of it from the south-west to the north-east, and a second, lower, crosses the north; between them lies Dzilidzar, enclosed on every side.

The prevailing wind is westerly. It arrives wet off the ocean and is wrung out on the western slopes, so the west coast carries broadleaf forest while the country behind the range is grass and thorn. Rainfall across the surveyed land runs from 115 mm in the driest basins to 2,434 mm on the windward ridges.

The highest ground is **Venisluz**, 4,889 m. 32 settlements were visited, holding some 474,600 people between them; 13 river systems were traced from mouth to source.

## The three tongues

Every name in this gazetteer is a compound of ordinary words, and every compound describes the place it is attached to. A town at a river's mouth is called *mouth-of-that-river*; a farm on wet ground is called *reed-field*. The names are therefore readable, and the reading is given in each entry.

What differs from place to place is how far the words have worn down. Each tongue has a hearth. Sound changes begin there and travel outward at the speed of ordinary traffic, which is to say they travel fast along a river and slowly over a pass. A town four days' walk from the hearth has taken up most of the changes; a valley behind the range has taken up only the oldest, and keeps forms the hearth abandoned generations ago. **The isoglosses on the map are drawn where the changes stopped, and they stop at the mountains.**

### Ammoric

Hearth at **Neilumhi** (43.32°N, 0.00°E). 13 of the surveyed settlements bear Ammoric names. Its country is called *Heetaveil*, 'far-land'.

Compounds are **head-initial** — the generic element stands first, which is why so many Ammoric names begin alike. Linking vowel *-a-*. 11 sound changes are recorded, in this order:

| # | change | rule |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | lenition-k | k weakens between vowels |
| 2 | raise-a | long a raises toward e |
| 3 | syncope | a vowel drops between two liquids |
| 4 | nasal-loss | a nasal before a stop leaves the vowel long |
| 5 | cluster | an awkward onset takes a prop vowel |
| 6 | spirantise | g becomes a breath between vowels |
| 7 | palatal | k fronts before i and e |
| 8 | final-devoice | final voiced stops harden |
| 9 | palatal-s | s hushes before front vowels |
| 10 | degemination | doubled consonants simplify |

## How this was made

The ground came first. A rough uplift field was subjected to repeated rounds of stream-power incision and hillslope diffusion until the drainage network organised itself; the valleys are where the water actually cut them.

Moisture was then advected across the finished terrain from the west, gaining water over sea and losing it on windward slopes, which produced the rainfall field. Vegetation follows from rainfall and temperature.

Settlements were sited by scoring every cell on fresh water, flat arable ground and shelter, then taking the best remaining spot repeatedly. Roads are least-effort walking paths over the real slope.

The names came last, and only from what was already there. Each is a compound of words true of its site, aged by the sound changes that had reached that far — where *far* is measured in walking effort, not in kilometres. Nothing about the languages was placed by hand except the phoneme inventories and the catalogue of possible changes.
